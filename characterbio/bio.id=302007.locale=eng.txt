<p>$[name] was born in Australia on a small farm, near the Victorian town of Mildura in $[birthDate]. His father was a cattle grazier and owned the property. He grew up making the five-mile walk to the nearest school and was an adventurous and often unruly student. He did poorly in his studies and could barely write his name when he left school. As soon as he could, he began working full time on his father's farm. He found the farm work even more boring than the schoolwork and left to find work in Ballarat, where he found work as a labourer. He lived in a poorly built apartment on the edge of town for five years. Eventually he was promoted to a carter, but that was as far as he rose. He found controlling the cart at first exciting, but it soon became monotonous. It was his job to cart the horse-droppings left on the streets everyday and take them to be put into fertiliser. In his free time, $[firstName] courted a rich girl who he tried to take to the best venues with his meagre earnings and pretend to be a rich man about town. This all collapsed when she saw him one evening doing his work, realised she had been lied to and never spoke to him again.</p>
<p>War broke out not even a day later, and out of anger and grief he went to enlist the moment he heard of it. $[firstName] joined the 7th Battalion of the 2nd Brigade, AIF, commanded by Colonel "Pompey" Elliot and was sent to Egypt for training. The British officers there disliked the weather, disliked the troops under their command and disliked $[name] most of all. Despite this, he was promoted to Sergeant, and when he saw his first aeroplane flying overhead near Cairo, he fell in love. He went to his Captain, who refused to transfer him. He then committed the grievous error of going over the Captain's head and went to Colonel Elliot. He was refused and was told he was too good a sergeant to be let go. The Captain heard of this conversation, and $[lastName] was surreptitiously demoted to Corporal. He continued to write sad love letters to the rich girl back home, but she did not reply to him. Eventually he gave up, two days before they left for the Dardanelles.</p>
<p>$[name]'s unit was amongst the first wave on the beach at Gallipoli, and he was mentioned in dispatches for leading a group of men against a machine gun post that had pinned down his Company. Because of his heroic actions, he was recommended for the Distinguished Service Medal. $[firstName] continued to serve at Gallipoli until the Battle of Lone Pine, when he was shot in the leg and evacuated. The injury was bad enough that $[firstName] was taken out of the Battalion, and it was then that he again asked for a transfer to aviation while hospitalized. What followed was an anxious month of waiting until his request was accepted. In September 1915, he was sent to France to train as a mechanic. $[firstName] was still limping slightly from his wound when he was selected to enter flight training by an officer who learned of his heroics at Gallipoli. His training went well, and he was promoted to $[startRank] upon his assignment to $[startSquadronName].</p>
