<p>$[name] was born to parents Richard and Cynthia on $[birthDate], who resided in Boston’s Fenway neighborhood. His father was a successful attorney, while his mother worked part time as a receptionist.</p>

<p>While he would eventually find his way into the pilot’s seat of the much-vaunted B-17 bomber and then a second career as a fighter pilot, like all young American boys he had aspirations far different from where the war would eventually take him.</p>

<p>From an early age, $[firstName] was taught the importance of a good education, saving for a rainy day, and the all-American love of baseball. His mother often remarked that he and his father spent more time at Fenway Park than they did at home, and during some summers her remark was not far from the truth. His father’s firm held a dozen first base-line seats in reserve, and he took his young son as often as he could, which was nearly once a week. When not at a game, he was often imitating his favorite players in the empty lots or playing in the streets with his friends in daily pickup baseball games.</p>

<p>Memories of sitting in the sun, eating hot dogs, and watching Lefty Grove work his magic were something that $[firstName] would hold dear for the rest of his life, especially after his father was killed in an automobile accident in November of 1941, barely a month before the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor. As it did with most boys his age, this act of aggression changed the course of his life and led to events both memorable, joyful, and tragic. The loss of his father underscored the anger that he felt that December and, losing all interest in his budding legal career, he found himself at an Air Corps recruiter’s office the following January.</p>

<p>$[firstName] would sometimes revert to those memories with his father and Fenway Park when sitting next to his co-pilot in his B-17, 29,000 feet over Germany. Missions consisted of very early mornings, followed by hours of boredom and fatigue, with moments of sheer terror and danger when the Luftwaffe showed up. He often envied the fighters, or “little friends” as they were called among the bomber crews. Like deadly angels they kept the enemy 109s and 190s at bay, and sometimes sent them hurtling toward the ground broken and burning, no longer a danger to his men and their mission.</p>

<p>$[firstName] often thought about what it must be like to be one of them, to have freedom, to actually fight back rather than be stuck like a canary in a cage. He was never sure why he had been chosen for bombers in the first place during training, but he had done his duty without a complaint. Unable to maneuver and unable to do anything but fly into the maw of the enemy and hope his little friends were successful, he had always felt in his heart that he belonged out there with the fighters.</p>

<p>So it was that on a sunny morning during a ‘down’ day when $[firstName] had two missions to go before the magic “25” that would finally send him home to Boston, that a young 2nd Lieutenant holding a clipboard made the rounds in the officer’s barracks offering bomber pilots the opportunity to enroll in fighter training after their bomber tour was up.</p>

<p>The general reaction was a collective eye roll, with a mumbling consensus around the small table of “thanks but no thanks, kid,” but $[firstName] remained quiet, with a cigarette dangling from his mouth, as the last breath of smoke escaped from the side from his lips.. His friend Cody Riggins, who was a short-timer himself with only three missions remaining, saw the look on his friend’s face and knew what he was thinking. “No, no..come on buddy, all you talk about is getting home and going to a Red Sox game!”</p>

<p>$[firstName] looked at his friend, mashing the cigarette in the ashtray and extinguishing it, then gazed up at the young officer. “The Sox can wait, Riggs. I’m going to get me a piece of those bastards. Where do I sign?”</p>
