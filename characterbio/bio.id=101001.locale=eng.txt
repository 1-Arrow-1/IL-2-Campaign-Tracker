<p>Born the third son of a Pacific Fleet officer stationed in Vladivostok in $[birthDate], $[name] was of significantly smaller stature than his brothers, but he showed an early aptitude for mathematics as well as great skill with tasks that relied upon manual dexterity. In late 1935, while attending the Far Eastern Polytechnic Institute, $[firstName] took a job at a local event that featured an aerial display including I-15 biplanes as well as the brand new I-16 monoplane. Having been enamored with aviation since he was a boy listening to stories of the Second Fatherland War, he would strike up conversations with the pilots, after they had landed and come in for refreshments. In particular, $[firstName] greatly impressed the squadron leader - a pilot named Muravitsky - with his knowledge of aviation, engines, and aerodynamics. A week later, a representative of the Far Eastern Council of People's Commissars visited him between classes and told him he had been given a great honor: he would be afforded the opportunity to join <i>Aeroflot</i>, which was paving the way for fast travel and transfer of goods throughout the massive Soviet Union.</p>
<p>$[firstName] attempted to continue his scholastic pursuits while he also worked diligently to perform his tasks for <i>Aeroflot</i>. He showed such promise, however, that he was taken into the flight training program within his first month, and so he was forced to discontinue his studies at the Institute. By the spring of 1937, he was flying a Kalinin K-5 passenger route from Khabarovsk to Okha on the island of Sakhalin. Though of his brothers he was neither the great bow-legged goalkeeper nor the tough-as-nails navy boxer, he had finally made his father proud.</p>
<p>In January 1941, $[name] was sent far away from his home and family to the other end of the world: Europe. The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact had reopened the sky lanes between Germany and the Soviet Union, and the demand for skilled pilots was at an all-time high. Quickly proving to his fellows that he was not some simple yokel from the Mongolian countryside, $[firstName] flew both the Tupolev PS-35 as well as the GAZ PS-84 from Moscow to Konigsberg, then on to Berlin and back again. It was an exciting and chaotic time filled with promise and energy; when he was not chasing clouds, he and his fellows were chasing drinks and ladies. The sky was the limit. However, it would not be long before world events would dramatically change his life.</p>
<p>In the early morning hours of June 22, 1941, $[firstName] and his fellows heard the radio reports of the German invasion as they reported for work at Shosseynaya Airport in Leningrad. Some chuckled that a great prank was being played. When they arrived at the airfield, it was readily apparent that it was no prank. Men and vehicles moved about in a rush. The Great Patriotic War had begun. Ordered to assemble on the tarmac, instead of their flight operations supervisor they were met by officers from the Red Army Air Forces and Naval Aviation. To a man, they were all being absorbed into the armed forces. The officer addressing the men kept his speech short and to the point: "Comrades, yesterday we carried mail to Germany. Today, we will carry bombs."</p>
